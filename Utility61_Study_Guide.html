01.01 Maps aka: Street Atlas, Paper Nav
What It Is
Physical street maps of your response district. Your digital backup plan when technology fails — and it will fail.
How To Use It
When GPS or the MDT loses signal, pull the relevant map, orient it to your direction of travel, and identify the nearest cross streets. Practice folding it back to your district zone so you're not wrestling a full atlas in a moving cab.
Why It's Needed
MDTs crash, cell service drops, and GPS satellites glitch. A paper map never loses battery. Knowing your district grid cold can save critical minutes on a working fire response.
Rookie Mistake

Don't rely exclusively on the MDT address display. Know your district's major roads, apartment complexes, and hydrant locations well enough that the map is just confirming what you already know.

01.02 Truck Loading Instruction Paper
What It Is
A reference sheet describing the proper loading procedures, weight distribution, and equipment placement for Utility 61.
Why It's Needed
Overloading or improperly distributing weight on a utility vehicle affects handling, braking, and suspension. This sheet keeps you operating within safe parameters — especially after gear has been pulled for an incident.
01.03 Patient Prepare Reports (Blank) aka: PCR, Patient Care Report forms
What It Is
Blank paper forms used to document patient information at a medical scene — vitals, complaint, treatment rendered, demographics.
How To Use It
Fill out completely at the scene or en route. Name, DOB, chief complaint, vitals over time, interventions, and your name/ID. Legibility matters — this becomes a legal document.
Why It's Needed
Documentation is part of patient care. When EMS arrives and takes over, your written report bridges the gap. In court or a QA review, if it's not written down, it didn't happen.
01.04 Vehicle Name Laminate — "Utility 61"
What It Is
A laminated card identifying this apparatus as "Utility 61." Used for staging identification, ICS documentation, and resource accountability at multi-unit incidents.
Why It's Needed
On a large incident with multiple apparatus, command needs to track resources by unit ID. This laminate also helps when staging in unfamiliar locations and communicating your position over the radio.
01.05 Spare Tire Lock Key
What It Is
Stored in a bag in the glove compartment. A keyed socket that unlocks the anti-theft lug nut protecting the spare tire mount underneath the vehicle.
Why It's Needed
Without this specific key, you cannot remove the spare — a flat tire on a response vehicle without it means calling for a tow and taking Utility 61 out of service. Know exactly where it is before you need it at 2 AM.
Critical Note

This key is in a bag — don't reach into the glove compartment looking for a bare key. If it goes missing, report it immediately. A missing key discovered during a roadside flat is a very bad day.

01.06 Road Flares (2x) aka: Fusees, Flares, Signal Flares
What It Is
Pyrotechnic warning devices that burn bright red for 15–30 minutes. Used for traffic control and scene visibility at roadway incidents.
How To Use It
  1. Remove cap from the end of the flare
  2. Scrape the cap against the rough tip — like striking a match
  3. Ignition is immediate — hold away from your face
  4. Place upright (or at an angle) on the pavement away from traffic
  5. Position upstream of the incident to warn approaching drivers
Why It's Needed
Secondary incidents from distracted drivers are a leading cause of firefighter injuries and deaths. Flares create an unmistakable warning zone that even a distracted driver will notice.
Also Known As
In NFPA training documents you'll see these called fusees. On scene you'll hear "throw some flares" or "get the fusees out." Same thing.
Safety Warning

Never use road flares near a fuel or gas leak. A lit flare near spilled fuel will ignite it instantly. At fuel-involved MVAs, use traffic cones or LED alternatives only. Also wear your roadway vest any time you're near moving traffic.

01.07 Tag Velcro — Accountability System aka: PAR Tags, Accountability Tags
What It Is
A two-piece velcro name tag system for tracking which firefighters are assigned to this apparatus. Driver puts their name tag upside-down. White velcro goes to the officer in command. Red velcro stays with the vehicle.
How To Use It
  1. Driver writes name on tag and places it upside-down (standard protocol so OIC reads it right-side up when taken)
  2. White piece → Officer in Command at the scene
  3. Red piece → Stays on the vehicle
  4. At end of incident, OIC returns white tag; accountability is complete
Why It's Needed
If Utility 61 is involved in a MAYDAY or personnel accountability report (PAR) is called, the OIC knows exactly who came off this rig. This system has found missing firefighters. It is not optional.
Never Skip This

Accountability tags are life-safety equipment. If you're driving, getting that tag to the OIC is as important as any tool you carry. White to OIC. Red stays with the rig. Name upside-down. Drill it until it's automatic.

03.05–07 Fuel Key / Prairie Path Key / Knox Box Key Knox Box key is on the wooden stick
Fuel Key
Authorizes fueling at department fuel pumps. Do not use this for personal vehicles. Unauthorized use is a terminable offense in most departments.
Prairie Path Key
Grants access to gated areas along the Prairie Path corridor in your district — for incidents along trails, underpasses, or restricted green space.

03.10 Nitrile Gloves aka: Exam Gloves, Medical Gloves, BSI Gloves
What It Is
Disposable nitrile (latex-free) examination gloves. Stored in multiple locations on Utility 61 — bench, back seat, and the bed — because you will reach for them at the start of every patient contact.
How To Use It
Don before any patient contact. Double-glove when dealing with blood, body fluids, or unknown substances. Remove without touching the contaminated outer surface — peel from the wrist, invert, dispose properly.
Why It's Needed
Body Substance Isolation (BSI) is your first line of defense. Every patient is a potential exposure. Every time, no exceptions, no shortcuts.
Universal Precautions

Gloves go on before you touch the patient. Always. Even if they're your neighbor, even if they "look clean," even if it's a minor call. The firefighters who've gotten Hepatitis C or HIV didn't think they were at risk either.

03.11–12 "Do Not Cross" Tape & "Fire" Tape aka: Scene Tape, Barrier Tape, Caution Tape
What It Is
Brightly colored plastic tape used to establish a scene perimeter. "Do Not Cross" keeps civilians out. "Fire" tape specifically marks fire department controlled areas. Neither is load-bearing — it's a visual boundary only.
How To Use It
String between vehicles, poles, trees, or any stable anchor point. Establish a perimeter large enough to work safely — you can always shrink it, but expanding it after curious bystanders have wandered in is chaos. Keep it taut and visible.
Why It's Needed
Scene control protects bystanders from hazards, protects evidence at fire investigations, keeps media and family from interfering with patient care, and gives your crew a defined workspace.
The Blanket Alternative

The document mentions using the bed blanket as a shield from the public — a good reminder that improvised scene management tools are fair game. A blanket held up by two firefighters creates visual privacy for a patient in a public space faster than stringing tape. Know your options.

04.01 Portable Officer Radios (2) — Green Holsters aka: Portables, HTs (Handie-Talkies), Portable Radios
What It Is
Two portable two-way radios stored in green holsters in the back seat. Primary communication tool for officers working away from the apparatus. Pre-programmed with Fire East (Channel 1) and Fire White (Channel 3) at minimum.
Channel Setup
  • Fire East = Channel 1 (first channel, default)
  • Fire White = Channel 3 (fireground operations)
  • Microphone button can change channels
  • Voice recognition: say "Hey you, It's Me" to activate
Radio Discipline
  • Do NOT scan channels when deployed
  • Keep volume low when in groups to avoid feedback/echo
  • Red button = emergency activation — only in a true MAYDAY
  • If activated accidentally: immediately say "ACCIDENTAL ACTIVATION"
How To Turn On & Scan
Power on, confirm channel, set volume appropriate to environment. To scan East and Fireground White: verify you're on Channel 1, press scan function per radio model, confirm both channels are active in scan list.
Emergency Button — Know This Cold

The red emergency button triggers a department-wide MAYDAY alarm. Every radio on the system hears it. Dispatch logs it. If you press it by mistake, immediately transmit "ACCIDENTAL ACTIVATION" — every second of silence after an accidental activation has dispatch rolling additional resources to find a firefighter who isn't in trouble. Don't freeze up.

04.02 AED — Automated External Defibrillator aka: Shock Box, Defib, AED
What It Is
A portable device that analyzes cardiac rhythm and, when appropriate, delivers an electrical shock to restore a normal heartbeat in ventricular fibrillation (V-Fib) or ventricular tachycardia (V-Tach) cardiac arrest. Also includes a pocket mask for rescue breathing.
Status Check
Screen should show GREEN, not red. Green = operational and ready. Red = fault, battery issue, or pad expiration. A red light means the AED is not ready to save a life — report it immediately and do not clear the rig.
How To Use It
  1. Power on — it will talk you through every step
  2. Bare the patient's chest — dry it if wet
  3. Apply pads per diagram (right clavicle / left side under armpit)
  4. Clear the patient — "I'm clear, you're clear, everyone clear!"
  5. Follow voice prompts — shock if advised, resume CPR if not
What's Inside
  • AED unit itself
  • Defibrillation pads (attached to chest)
  • Pocket mask for rescue breathing
Every Second Counts

For every minute without defibrillation in V-Fib arrest, survival decreases by approximately 10%. Your job is to get those pads on and shock as fast as possible. Practice opening the case, removing pads, and placing them — timed. Get it under 60 seconds.

06.01 Halligan Bar aka: Halligan, Hooligan Tool, The Bar
What It Is
A multi-function forcible entry tool with three distinct ends: the adze (flat chisel-like end), the pick (pointed spike), and the fork (two-pronged fork end). Considered the single most important forcible entry tool in the fire service.
Three Ends — Three Uses
  • Adze: Driven between door frame and door to create a gap; also used as a prying platform
  • Pick: Punches through hollow core doors, shatter glass, or opens lids
  • Fork: Slips around door jamb to pop the lock bolt; great for inward-opening doors
Why It's Needed
Forcible entry — opening locked structures — is a core fireground skill. A Halligan paired with a flathead axe (the "irons") is the standard forced entry combination used by departments worldwide.
The Irons

The Halligan + Flathead Axe pairing is called "the irons." One person holds the Halligan, one swings the axe to drive it. Practice this as a team skill — it's a two-person operation. Trying to drive a Halligan solo costs time and accuracy.

Watch: Halligan Forcible Entry Techniques
06.02 Flathead Axe aka: Flat-Head, Striking Tool, Half of the Irons
What It Is
An axe with a flat poll (back) and a sharpened blade. The flat poll makes it ideal for striking (driving a Halligan). The blade cuts through light materials, wood, and drywall.
How To Use It
As a striking tool: flat poll drives the Halligan adze into a door gap with controlled, accurate swings. As a cutting tool: blade for through-the-lock, roof ventilation, or breaking materials. Keep blade sharp — a dull axe is a dangerous axe.
Why It's Needed
The flathead is the "driver" to the Halligan's "wedge." Together they're the fastest, most reliable forced entry combination in the fire service — no hydraulics, no power needed.
06.03 Closet Pike Pole with D-Ring Handle aka: Short Pike, D-Handle Pike, Rubbish Hook
What It Is
A shorter version of the standard pike pole, featuring a D-ring handle for controlled pulling. The hook end pulls down ceilings, opens walls, and drags burning debris out of hidden spaces.
How To Use It
Insert hook into ceiling material and pull down to expose hidden fire in the void space above. The D-ring handle gives better grip when pulling with force. The shorter length makes it practical in tight hallways and closets where a standard 6-foot pole won't maneuver.
Why It's Needed
Fire loves to travel in wall and ceiling void spaces unseen. "Overhauling" — pulling down ceilings and walls after the main body is knocked down — finds hidden fire before it re-ignites. Without this tool, fire hides and rekindling occurs.
Know This for Your Exam

The document specifically mentions the D-ring handle as a test item. When asked to identify the pike pole on the rig, note the D-ring — it distinguishes the closet pike from a standard hook. The examiner is checking whether you actually looked at the tool, not just the list.

06.04 AC Hot Stick aka: Voltage Detector Stick, Non-Contact Voltage Tester
What It Is
An insulated pole-mounted non-contact voltage detector. When energized, detects the presence of AC electrical current without physical contact with the conductor. Has 3 settings that must all be demonstrated to function correctly.
How To Use It
  1. Power on the unit — verify all 3 settings cycle correctly
  2. Hold the tip near (not touching) a suspected energized conductor
  3. Audible and/or visual alarm activates when AC voltage is present
  4. No alarm = not detecting voltage (not a guarantee of zero volts)
Why It's Needed
Downed power lines, flooded structures with energized water, or vehicles in contact with electrical infrastructure are all potentially lethal without confirmation of de-energization. The Hot Stick tells you before you touch.
Critical Safety — Electricity Kills Without Warning

A Hot Stick that isn't working won't alarm even if voltage is present. That's why you test all 3 settings before approaching a suspected electrical hazard. A unit that powers on but doesn't cycle through all settings is not reliable — treat it as broken and report it. Never assume a downed line is de-energized until the utility confirms it.

06.05 Jumper Cables aka: Booster Cables, Jump Cables
Color Code
Red = Positive (+)  |  Black = Negative (−)
For the dead car: red cable goes on the ground (negative terminal) as the final connection to avoid sparks near the battery.
Proper Connection Sequence
  1. Red to dead battery (+)
  2. Red to good battery (+)
  3. Black to good battery (−)
  4. Black to ground on dead car (not the dead battery) — unpainted metal, away from battery
  5. Start the good car, then the dead car
  6. Remove in reverse order
Why Ground, Not Battery?

Batteries off-gas hydrogen — a flammable, explosive gas. Connecting the final clamp directly to the dead battery can spark and ignite that gas. Connecting to an engine ground point (unpainted metal) away from the battery makes that same spark happen safely away from the gas source. This is the step most civilians get wrong. Don't be them.

06.06 Measuring Wheel aka: Distance Wheel, Surveyor's Wheel, Walking Wheel
What It Is
A rolling measurement device that counts distance traveled. Used specifically for accident reporting — measuring skid marks, point of impact, vehicle final rest positions, and debris fields.
How To Use It
Reset the counter to zero, walk the distance with the wheel rolling along the surface, read the measurement. Accurate to within a foot over long distances — far more reliable than a tape measure at an accident scene.
06.07 SurpressAll Unit — Gas Grenade aka: Odor Suppressor, Scene Deodorizer
What It Is
A deployable odor-suppression device used to control and neutralize strong odors at scenes — decomposition, sewage backups, or post-fire smoke odor in occupied structures.
Why It's Needed
Scene management includes managing the environment. Overwhelming odors compromise responder focus, affect civilian wellbeing, and can indicate hazardous gases. The SurpressAll helps neutralize odors while you investigate the source.
Do Not Mask — Investigate

Never use odor suppression before confirming the source of the odor. A gas odor needs to be identified and neutralized at the source — not covered up. Suppressing a natural gas odor without addressing the leak is dangerous.

06.08 Hydrant Wrench aka: Spanner Wrench, Hydrant Spanner
What It Is
A dedicated wrench sized to fit fire hydrant operating nuts and caps. Used to open and close hydrant valves and remove/replace discharge caps.
How To Use It
Fit the wrench over the pentagonal operating nut. Turn counterclockwise to open (remember: "righty tighty, lefty loosey" — except hydrant caps, which may be reverse-threaded — know your hydrant type). Never fully open a hydrant without a charged hose in place.
Know Your Hydrants

Some hydrant caps and some operating nuts in different districts may be reverse-threaded. When in doubt, have an experienced driver show you the hydrants in your response area. Two seconds of confirmation prevents stripping a hydrant during a working fire.

06.10 ABC Fire Extinguisher (Dry Chemical) aka: Dry Chem, ABC Extinguisher, Monoammonium Phosphate Unit
What It Is
A pressurized extinguisher using monoammonium phosphate dry chemical agent. Rated for Class A (ordinary combustibles), Class B (flammable liquids), and Class C (energized electrical equipment) fires.
PASS Technique
  • Pull the pin
  • Aim at the base of the fire
  • Squeeze the handle
  • Sweep side to side at the base
The 40-Rule
Stand at least 40 feet back when approaching a vehicle fire. A vehicle fire can produce sudden tire explosions, fuel ignition, and airbag deployments — all potentially lethal. The 40-foot rule gives you reaction time.
Why It's Needed
The ABC extinguisher is your fast-attack suppression tool for incipient (small, early-stage) fires before a hose line is deployed, vehicle fires, and electrical incidents.
Know Your Extinguisher Types — This Is a Test Item

The document lists TWO extinguishers: one in the bed (loose tools) and an ABC Dry Chem Extinguisher stored in the bed bags section — containing monoammonium phosphate. Know what each type fights and what chemical it uses. The 40-foot rule applies to vehicle fires specifically.

07.01 Red Pack — First In / Jump Pack aka: Trauma Bag, First-In Bag, Jump Bag — Red = Trauma
The 4 B's — What This Bag Is For
Bleeding · Burns · Broken · Birthing
If it's trauma, this is your bag. It's called the First-In bag because it goes with the first person into the scene.
Contents
  • Shears (for cutting clothing)
  • Glucose level measuring device
  • Medical drugs and Narcan (naloxone)
  • Personal protective equipment (PPE)
  • Sharps container
  • BP cuff (blood pressure)
  • Splints
  • Tourniquet
  • Markers
Key Items Explained
Narcan (Naloxone): Reverses opioid overdose. If they're unresponsive and you suspect opioids — use it. Side effects of giving Narcan to someone who doesn't need it are minimal. Side effects of not giving it to someone who does are fatal.

Tourniquet: For life-threatening extremity hemorrhage. Apply 2–3 inches above the wound, tighten until bleeding stops, note the time. Modern doctrine: tourniquet early and aggressively on severe extremity bleeds.
Shears — First Tool Out

You cannot assess what you can't see. Shears cut through clothing, seatbelts, and gear to expose the patient. Cut first, ask questions later. A patient who needs a new shirt is alive to complain about it. Don't let modesty or hesitation delay assessment of a trauma patient.

07.02 Green Pack — Airway Pack aka: Airway Bag, Breathing Bag — Green = Breathing/Airway
One Word — What This Bag Is For
Breathing. If they can't breathe, this is your bag. Color-coded green because oxygen and go. Grab this when the airway is the priority.
Contents
  • Breathing equipment (various)
  • Oxygen tank
  • AMBU masks — Artificial Manual Breathing Unit
  • BVM — Bag, Valve, Mask
  • Pediatric breathing tubes
  • Intubation tubes / airway tubes
  • Suction device
  • Nasal Cannula tubes
  • Oral and nasal airways (OPA/NPA)
  • PPE
  • Advanced Airway equipment
Acronyms — Know These
AMBU = Artificial Manual Breathing Unit
Used to manually ventilate a patient — the mask you hold to the face to "bag" someone.

BVM = Bag, Valve, Mask
The complete device: squeeze bag → forces air through one-way valve → into mask → into lungs. Used for rescue breathing and assisted ventilation.

OPA = Oropharyngeal Airway (oral)
NPA = Nasopharyngeal Airway (nasal)
Color Code to Remember

Red bag = Trauma (Bleeding, Burns, Broken, Birthing)
Green bag = Airway/Breathing
When you arrive on scene and split tasks, one person takes Red and one takes Green — you've covered the two biggest life threats.

07.03 Red Bag w/ Black Zipper — Water Rescue aka: Throwbag Bag, Water Rescue Bag
Contents
  • One throw rope (throwbag — rope stuffed in a bag for fast deployment)
  • One life preserver vest (PFD — Personal Flotation Device)
How Throwback Works
  1. Grab the bag, hold the rope end yourself
  2. Throw the bag to the victim — the rope pays out as it travels
  3. Victim grabs the bag/rope — you pull them in
  4. Brace yourself and never let go of your end
"Throwback" = after a rescue, repack the bag by stuffing rope back in (not coiling) for fast re-deployment.
Don't Become the Second Victim

Never enter the water without a PFD and a tethered line. Reach, throw, row, go — in that order. Jumping into water to save someone without equipment has killed more rescuers than victims. Throw the rope first.

07.04 Black Plastic Case — MSA Airpack aka: SCBA, Self-Contained Breathing Apparatus, Air Pack, Scott Pack
Contents
  • One Airpack (SCBA unit)
  • Air bottle (stored in the black case)
  • Medium-sized face piece
What It Is
A self-contained breathing apparatus — a harness-mounted compressed air cylinder with regulator and face piece that provides breathable air in smoke, IDLH (Immediately Dangerous to Life or Health) atmospheres, or oxygen-deficient environments.
Why It's Needed
This is a backup or auxiliary SCBA. An on-scene firefighter who runs low on air, loses their primary SCBA, or needs to stay in the hot zone while their pack is serviced can use this unit. It's also available for situations requiring air in environments not covered by apparatus-mounted SCBAs.
Check the Air Pressure — Every Shift

A low-pressure air bottle in the MSA case is a useless air bottle in an emergency. Verify the cylinder pressure on every shift check. A full bottle reads 4,500 PSI on most modern SCBAs. Below 90% full, flag it for refill.

07.05 Blue Bag w/ Black Straps — Cervical Collar Bag aka: C-Collar Bag, Spinal Immobilization Bag, Stab Kit
Contents
  • Stabilization hardware and two styles of neck braces (cervical collars)
  • Foam head-locks
  • Red/Orange with white stripe bag = Mass Casualties / Triage Tag Bag
  • Orange bag with black zipper = Traction materials (straps, tubes for splints)
Cervical Collar Sizing — Two Methods
Method 1 (Measurement):
Measure with fingers along the blue section of the collar — from bottom of the shoulder blade to the top of the jaw. The number of finger-widths = collar size.

Method 2 (Pin-to-Chin):
Move the black peg into the round hole to form the jaw rest. This is the "pin to chin" collar — the peg gives chin support for proper immobilization.
Foam Head-Locks
Unroll foam blocks on the backboard, position between the board and the patient's head on both sides. Tape across the forehead and across the collar to the board — this completes full spinal immobilization. Without the head blocks, the collar alone doesn't prevent lateral movement.
Triage Tag System — Mass Casualty
The Red/Orange bag with white stripe is your mass casualty triage bag. Uses a 4-color system to classify patient priority:

■ RED — Immediate: life-threatening, can survive with immediate treatment
■ YELLOW — Delayed: serious but stable, can wait
■ GREEN — Minor: "walking wounded," lowest priority
■ BLACK — Expectant: deceased or unsurvivable injuries
C-Collar — Sizing Matters

A cervical collar that's too large won't immobilize. One that's too small can hyperextend the neck and worsen a cervical injury. Take 10 seconds to measure. It changes outcomes. Practice the finger-measurement method on your crew members during downtime so it's automatic on scene.

ERG
08.00 ERG — Emergency Response Guidebook aka: ERG, The Orange Book, The Hazmat Bible
What ERG Stands For
Emergency Response Guidebook — published by the U.S. DOT. Your first-responder reference for hazardous materials incidents. It tells you what the hazard is, what to do, how far to stay back, and who to call.
Yellow Pages — By Number
Lists materials in numerical order by UN/NA identification number (4-digit code). Find the number → get the guide number → go to Orange Pages.

Green highlighting in Yellow Pages = Toxic Inhalation Hazard or Dangerous Water Reactive Material. These have evacuation distances in the Green Section.
Blue Pages — By Name
Lists materials alphabetically by name. Same outcome — leads you to the guide number and the Orange Pages.

Green highlighting in Blue Pages = material has evacuation distances in the Green Section.
Orange Pages — Response Guides
The action section. For each guide number:
  • Potential health hazards
  • Fire/explosion risk
  • Emergency response procedures
  • Required PPE
  • Evacuation procedures
  • First aid guidance
  • Spill and fire control measures
Green Pages — Evacuation Distances
For Toxic-by-Inhalation materials. Distances vary by:
  • Small spill vs. Large spill (over 50 gallons = large)
  • Day vs. Night (atmospheric conditions differ)
If you're setting up a perimeter at a hazmat scene, start here for distance guidance.
UN vs. NA Numbers
UN Numbers: International, start with "UN" — used worldwide. Example: UN1203 = Gasoline.

NA Numbers: North America only (USA, Canada, Mexico), start with "NA." Used for materials regulated in North America that may not have an international UN designation. Example: NA1993 = Combustible liquids.
ERG Flowchart — How to Use It
  1. Do you know the UN/NA ID number? → Search Yellow Pages
  2. Do you know the material name? → Search Blue Pages
  3. Do you see a placard or label? → Refer to Pages 8-9
  4. Do you see a rail car or road trailer? → Refer to Pages 10-15
  5. None of the above? → Use Guide 111 (default general hazmat guide)
  6. All roads lead to → Determine the Guide Number → Orange Pages
Back Pages — Hazmat Phone Numbers

The back pages contain critical phone numbers including CHEMTREC (1-800-424-9300) — the 24-hour hazmat emergency hotline. If your ERG doesn't give you what you need, CHEMTREC connects you with chemical manufacturers and hazmat experts in real time. Know this number exists before you need it.

09.01 Handrail Bar & Step Setup
Setup Sequence
  1. Pull down the tailgate
  2. Pull the bar up — make sure it locks
  3. Pull the step out from the top of the tailgate
  4. Flip the step out to deployed position
Closing Sequence
  1. Make sure the bed cover is at least one section open
  2. Close the tailgate
  3. Close the bed cover (confirm it closes fully)
Order Matters

Closing the tailgate before the bed cover is open one section can trap or damage the bed cover mechanism. Always confirm the cover is open first. And always confirm the cover is fully closed before moving the apparatus — loose covers at highway speed are equipment-destroying events.

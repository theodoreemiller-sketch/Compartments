Hose Bays — Front, Side/Middle & Rear

The lifeblood of engine operations. Know every load, every size, every connection.
💡 ORIENTATION TIP
Hose bays are not "compartments" — they're open bays that hold hose loads. Some are preconnected (ready to pull and go), some are static (used to supply or extend). Know which is which before someone hollers for a line.
FRONT HOSE BAY
100 ft Trash Line — 1¾" Preconnected
What It Is
A 100-foot preconnected 1¾" attack line stored in the front bumper area of the engine. "Trash" refers to its use on quick, small, or "throwaway" fires — dumpsters, car fires, small brush, rubbish. It's fast to deploy, usually with a fog nozzle, and designed for getting water on a small fire without pulling a big preconnect.
How to Use It
  1. Identify the trash line in the front bumper bay.
  2. Grab the nozzle end and pull while walking toward the fire — the hose will flake out behind you.
  3. Signal the engineer that the line is charged once positioned.
  4. Bleed air from the nozzle before advancing into the fire area.
  5. Use a straight stream or narrow fog pattern depending on conditions.
Why It's Needed
Speed. A dumpster fire or car fire doesn't need a 200 ft preconnect. The trash line is your fast-draw for anything small. It keeps your big lines reserved for working structure fires and avoids making a mess of a preconnect for a two-minute knock.
Also Known As
Bumper line, trash line, booster line (though "booster" technically refers to a tank-fed hard-rubber reel line on older rigs), front line.
▶ WATCH IT IN ACTION
Search YouTube: "trash line bumper line deployment fire" — look for your department's SOG-compatible method.
25 ft 5-Inch Supply Hose (Front)
What It Is
A short 25-foot section of 5-inch large-diameter hose (LDH) stored in the front hose bay. Five-inch is the highway between the hydrant and your engine — it moves a massive volume of water compared to 2½" or 3" hose.
How to Use It
This short section is typically used to make up the final connection from a hydrant to the engine's steamer inlet, or to bridge a short gap between another section and the engine. Connect Storz-to-Storz or use the steamer-to-Storz adapter in your hydrant bag. Make sure the Storz locking lugs click and lock before the engineer opens the hydrant.
Why It's Needed
When the engine can't get perfectly positioned at a hydrant, this short section bridges the gap without wasting a full hose load. It also gives you a ready piece to connect to a supply line when you need flow fast.
Also Known As
LDH (large-diameter hose), five-inch, supply hose, soft suction (outdated term), big line.
⚠ CRITICAL SAFETY
Never stand over or step on charged 5" hose. A rupture or failed Storz connection under pressure will injure or kill you. Also: Storz couplings must be fully locked — a partially seated coupling will blow off under pressure.
Storz-to-Steamer Adapter (Front Bay)
What It Is
A fitting that converts the Storz (sexless, quarter-turn) fitting on modern LDH hose to the threaded "steamer" (large-diameter threaded) fitting on older hydrants and older engine inlets. The "steamer connection" is the large-diameter inlet on a hydrant, named after the days when steam-powered fire engines connected here.
How to Use It
Thread the female threaded end onto the hydrant steamer port (hand-tight, then a quarter turn with the spanner), then connect the Storz side of your 5" hose. Always use the rubber mallet to ensure the Storz seats fully before charging.
Why It's Needed
Hydrant compatibility. Some hydrants in your district may still have the older threaded steamer connections. Without this adapter, you cannot connect modern Storz LDH to those hydrants.
Also Known As
Storz adapter, steamer adapter, LDH adapter, Storz-to-thread.
SIDE / MIDDLE HOSE BAY — CROSSLAYS
1¾" Crosslay Preconnects ×2 — 200 ft each, Fog Nozzle
What It Is
Two 200-foot preconnected 1¾" attack lines loaded in the side hose bay, crossing the truck so they can be pulled from either side. Each is equipped with a combination/fog nozzle. The 1¾" is the primary attack line for interior structural firefighting — the one you'll spend most of your career pulling.
How to Use It
  1. On arrival, the nozzle firefighter grabs the nozzle and flake out enough hose to reach the entry point.
  2. Announce "pulling the crosslay" and shoulder-load or flake hose toward the door.
  3. The engineer charges the line on your signal or the company officer's order.
  4. Bleed air at the nozzle — a line full of air is sluggish and unpredictable.
  5. Team advances with the nozzle firefighter controlling flow and the backup firefighter managing hose drag.
Why It's Needed
1¾" flows approximately 150–200 GPM — enough to knock down and control a working room-and-contents fire. It's maneuverable enough to navigate stairwells, hallways, and tight spaces. This is your bread-and-butter line.
Also Known As
Inch-and-three-quarter, inch-and-three, the crosslay, the preconnect, the attack line, the one-three-quarter.
🔰 ROOKIE MISTAKE
Pulling too much hose before the fire — you'll end up with a pile of uncharged spaghetti in the front yard. Estimate your stretch, pull a little extra, and flake it toward the door in a straight line so water flows easily. Kinks kill your flow and your time.

2½" Crosslay Preconnect 200 ft — Smooth Bore Nozzle
What It Is
A 200-foot preconnected 2½" attack line with a smooth bore (solid stream) nozzle, loaded in the side bay. The 2½" is your heavy-duty attack line — more flow, more reach, more knockdown power, and a lot more hose to manage.
How to Use It
  1. Typically requires two firefighters minimum — one on the nozzle, one backing up.
  2. Pull and flake as with the 1¾, but anticipate more weight and stiffer hose.
  3. Smooth bore nozzles have no bail — you open and close by turning the nozzle tip. Know your smooth bore nozzle operation cold.
  4. Coordinate with your engineer — this line demands more pump pressure and flow.
Why It's Needed
The 2½" flows 250–300 GPM. You need it for: large commercial fires, defensive operations, exposure protection, and any time a 1¾" just won't move enough water. It also reaches further with a solid stream than any fog nozzle.
Also Known As
Two-and-a-half, the big line, the master, the deuce-and-a-half, the two-and-a-half-inch preconnect.
⚠ CRITICAL SAFETY
A charged 2½" at operating pressure will knock you off your feet if you're not braced and ready. Never open the smooth bore nozzle without your feet planted and your partner on the hose behind you. This is not a one-person line in most situations.

Blitz Fire Line 300 ft — 3" Preconnected
What It Is
A 300-foot preconnected 3" line designed to supply a portable master stream device called the BlitzFire (or "Blitz" nozzle). The BlitzFire is a self-supporting oscillating monitor that you can set on the ground pointed at a fire and walk away from — it delivers a high-volume stream without requiring a firefighter to hold it.
How to Use It
  1. Pull the Blitz Fire line from the rear bay, deploying toward the target.
  2. Set up the BlitzFire monitor device at the end of the line — it mounts on the coupling.
  3. Aim the nozzle at the target before charging.
  4. Engineer charges the line; the BlitzFire self-oscillates once flowing.
  5. The nozzle that fits both the deck gun and the BlitzFire is in Compartment 1 (Engineer's comp) toolbox.
Why It's Needed
High-volume exterior attack, defensive operations, and exposure protection when you can't safely put people inside. The BlitzFire can flow 500+ GPM, and because it self-oscillates, it frees up personnel for other tasks.
Also Known As
BlitzFire, blitz line, ground monitor, portable master stream, deck gun line (when supplying the deck gun instead).

High Rise Pack & 150 ft 1¾" Line
What It Is
A prepackaged bundle of hose and fittings designed specifically for firefighting above the reach of ground ladder and aerial operations — typically any fire above the 7th floor. The pack contains: a short length of 1¾" hose, a 90-degree connector, a T-connector with pressure gauge, and vise grips to turn a standpipe valve. The 150 ft preconnected 1¾" line is stored alongside it.
How to Use It
  1. Carry the high-rise pack into the stairwell of the building.
  2. Stage one floor below the fire floor.
  3. Connect your hose to the standpipe outlet using the fittings in the pack.
  4. Use the pressure gauge T-fitting to monitor residual pressure — standpipe pressure can be dangerously high or dangerously low.
  5. Use the vise grips on stubborn or stripped standpipe valve handles.
  6. The 90-degree connector allows you to connect to a standpipe outlet positioned against a wall without kinking the hose.
Why It's Needed
High-rise fires are a fundamentally different type of operation. You can't lay supply lines up the side of a 20-story building. The building's built-in standpipe system becomes your water supply, and this pack gives you everything needed to tap into it safely and efficiently.
Also Known As
High-rise pack, standpipe kit, standpipe pack, high-rise bundle.
⚠ CRITICAL SAFETY
Standpipe systems can deliver water at pressures far exceeding what your attack line can safely handle — some operate at 200+ PSI at low floors. Always use your pressure gauge fitting and a pressure-reducing device or gated wye. A hose charged at 200 PSI will be uncontrollable and can rupture, injuring your crew.

Compartment 1 — Engineer's Compartment

Driver's Side · 3 Shelves · Water supply fittings, tools, hydrant gear
💡 CONTEXT
This is the engineer's kingdom. It lives on the driver's side and holds virtually every fitting, nozzle, and adapter needed to supply and control water operations. Know this compartment inside out — the engineer will ask you to grab things out of here, and hesitation wastes time.
TOP SHELF — MAINTENANCE & ENGINEER'S TOOLS
1" Green Garden Hose
What It Is
A small-diameter garden hose used for utility tasks at the station or on scene — washing down equipment, flushing lines, miscellaneous small-scale water tasks. Not a firefighting tool.
Why It's Needed
Convenience. Post-incident equipment cleanup, decontamination rinse, minor utility uses on scene. Sometimes used to top off the tank slowly at a fill station.
Also Known As
Garden hose, utility hose, small line.
Black Tool Box — Engineer's Maintenance Kit
What It Is
A maintenance and service toolbox carried specifically for keeping the apparatus and equipment in working order. Contents include: grease, screwdrivers (×2), needle-nose pliers, carb cleaner, food-grade silicone lubricant, heat gun with attachments, lube stick, Storz connectors (one is a reducer, 30-degree angle), a nozzle that fits the deck gun and BlitzFire, small utility flags (pink flag on wire, 12"), and an airpack.
Why It's Needed
Apparatus and equipment don't always cooperate. The engineer needs to be able to service equipment in the field — lubricate couplings, adjust fittings, clear a stuck nozzle, or mark a utility hazard with a flag. The food-grade silicone is specifically for lubricating rubber gaskets in hose couplings without degrading them (petroleum-based lubricants destroy EPDM gaskets). The heat gun can be used to thaw frozen couplings in winter operations.
Key Items Highlighted
Carb Cleaner: Solvent for cleaning fuel deposits from small engine carburetors (pump motor, saw, fan).

Lube Stick: Solid lubricant bar for keeping hose couplings turning freely.

Storz Connectors (with reducer): Adapters at a 30-degree angle to help connect hose at an awkward angle to a hydrant without kinking.

Utility Flags: Small pink marking flags used to mark hazards, utility shut-offs, or victim locations visible in grass or debris.
Also Known As
Engineer's box, maintenance kit, toolbox, service kit.
🔰 ROOKIE MISTAKE
Using petroleum-based (WD-40, motor oil) lubricants on hose gaskets. This degrades the rubber and causes leaks. Always use food-grade silicone or the purpose-made lube stick on gaskets and couplings.
MIDDLE SHELF — NOZZLES, SPANNERS & FITTINGS
Double-Ended Spanners ×2 — Storz & Hose Thread
What It Is
A spanner wrench with two different ends: one for Storz quarter-turn couplings and one for threaded (National Standard Thread) hose couplings. It allows you to tighten or loosen both types of connections with a single tool. The double-ended design means you always have the right end available.
How to Use It
Engage the appropriate end in the lug recesses of the coupling and apply leverage to tighten (clockwise) or loosen (counterclockwise). Hand-tight is the starting point — spanner tight is the target. For Storz, engage the lug and push to turn a quarter turn to seat and lock.
Why It's Needed
Hose couplings that aren't fully seated will leak or blow off under pressure. A spanner gives you the mechanical advantage to ensure a water-tight connection, especially after couplings have been swelling from water exposure.
Also Known As
Spanner, spanner wrench, hose wrench, coupling wrench.
Small House Spanners with Gas Turn-Off Notch ×2
What It Is
Smaller spanner wrenches sized for residential/commercial hose connections, with an additional notch or slot cut into the body specifically designed to engage and turn a residential gas meter shutoff valve.
Why It's Needed
Dual-purpose utility. On structure fires, you need to control ignition sources — that means getting gas meters shut off. These spanners let you do both jobs (secure hose connections AND shut gas) with one tool.
Also Known As
House spanner, gas shut-off wrench, combo spanner.
⚠ CRITICAL SAFETY
Shutting the gas is a utility company job at confirmed gas leaks — don't do it unless your department's SOG says to and you're certain of the action. Turning a gas meter off incorrectly or at the wrong valve can cause an explosion or leave the building without heat during dangerous conditions. Know your SOP.
Wye Ball Valves — 2½" to 1½" ×2
What It Is
A Y-shaped fitting that splits one 2½" inlet into two 1½" outlets, each controlled by its own ball valve. This lets you supply two separate 1½" or 1¾" attack lines from a single 2½" supply line, with individual shutoff control for each branch.
How to Use It
Connect the 2½" female inlet to your supply line coupling. Connect 1¾" attack lines to each 1½" outlet using reducers if needed. Open each ball valve individually to flow water to each attack line. Close one side to redirect all flow to a single line.
Why It's Needed
Water supply flexibility. If you're running two lines off a single supply — which happens in complex or multi-room fires — the wye lets you control them independently. You can also shut one line while keeping the other flowing, which is critical for crew safety during line changes.
Also Known As
Wye, gated wye, ball valve wye, two-and-a-half wye, water thief (when it's a 2½ to 1½ + 2½ configuration).
▶ WATCH IT IN ACTION
YouTube: "gated wye fire hose operation"
Fog Nozzles ×2 & Smooth Bore Nozzles ×1 + yellow tip ×1
What They Are
Fog Nozzle (Combination Nozzle): An adjustable nozzle that produces patterns from a narrow straight stream all the way to a wide 60-degree fog cone. The yellow-tip nozzle is a variation with an identifying color code.

Smooth Bore Nozzle: A fixed-opening nozzle that produces a solid cylindrical stream. No adjustability — what you see is what you get. Delivers maximum reach and maximum GPM at a given pressure.
How to Use Them
Fog: Twist the bail or rotating ring to adjust from straight stream to wide fog. Use straight stream for penetration and reach; use narrow fog for gas cooling and protection; use wide fog pattern only with good ventilation — in an unventilated room, a wide fog pattern can steam-burn occupants and crews.

Smooth Bore: Open the nozzle fully (it's a ball valve or turn-to-open design depending on the nozzle). Direct the stream. There's no adjustment — the tip diameter determines your flow rate. Common sizes: 7/8", 1", 1⅛" for 1¾" lines.
Why They're Needed
Different fires call for different water application. Smooth bore nozzles are preferred by many departments for interior attack because of their reach, penetration, and reduced steam production. Fog nozzles are preferred by others for their versatility. The extra nozzles on Comp 1's middle shelf are your spares and quick-swaps.
Also Known As
Smooth bore: solid bore, solid stream nozzle, shutoff, tip. Fog: combo nozzle, combination nozzle, adjustable fog, fog tip.
⚠ CRITICAL SAFETY
Never open a fog nozzle to a wide pattern in an enclosed, unventilated space. Steam burns are catastrophic and happen fast. The fog pattern also disrupts the thermal layering that gives you breathable (cooler) air near the floor.
Reducers & Adapters — Middle Shelf Assortment
What They Are
A collection of threaded fittings that adapt between different hose diameters. On the middle shelf you have: 2 small reducers, 1 reducer (1¾" to 1"), 4 reducers (2½" female to 1¾" male), 1 steamer-to-Storz adapter, and 1 cap for 1¾". These are the puzzle pieces of water supply — they make sure any hose can connect to any other.
Why They're Needed
Fire scene hose connections are rarely textbook-perfect. You'll encounter hydrant outlets that don't match your hose, supply lines that need to feed a smaller attack line, or a capped outlet that needs to be opened. These fittings solve those problems in seconds.
Also Known As
Reducers, adapters, fittings, couplings; specifically the steamer-to-Storz is called a "Storz adapter" or "LDH adapter."
💡 STUDY TIP
During compartment check, pick up each fitting and say aloud: "This is a [size] female to [size] male reducer — it goes between [X] and [Y]." Connecting name to function to application is how you remember under stress.
Hose Thread Connectors — 2½" and 1¾" Sets 6 each
What They Are
Sets of threaded hose couplings for making extensions, repairs, or non-standard connections. Each set of 6 contains:
• 2 Female-to-Female (F2F) — connects two male ends
• 2 Male-to-Male (M2M) — connects two female ends
• 2 CFD (Chicago Fire Department) thread fittings — for compatibility with neighboring departments or specialized apparatus
Why They're Needed
Mutual aid interoperability and field problem-solving. Thread standards vary by department and by hose generation. Having F2F and M2M adapters means you can always make a connection regardless of what ends you're working with. CFD fittings ensure compatibility when working joint operations.
Also Known As
F2F, M2M, double-female, double-male, sex changers (informal), connectors, couplings.
BOTTOM SHELF — HYDRANT BAG & 5" HOSE
Hydrant Bag — Complete Contents
What It Is
The hydrant bag is your one-stop kit for connecting to, flowing from, and managing a hydrant. It goes with the firefighter walking the hydrant on every supply lay. Contents: 2 hydrant wrenches, 2 steamer-to-Storz adapters, 2 small spanners with gas valve notch, 2 wedges, 1¾" gate valve, Storz-to-1¾" female adapter, 3 double-ended spanners, rubber mallet, pipe wrench, and a rope to move hose.
Key Items Explained
Hydrant Wrench: Pentagon-socket wrench that fits the operating nut on a fire hydrant. This is how you open and close the hydrant — do not open a hydrant without one or you'll be improvising with a crescent wrench and someone will get hurt.

Wedges: Wooden or rubber chocks placed in hose lays where the hose crosses curbs, thresholds, or goes around corners — protects the hose jacket from abrasion and keeps it from crimping at sharp angles.

1¾" Gate Valve: An inline shutoff valve that allows you to stop water flow without signaling the engineer. Useful for adding hose sections without shutting down the pump.

Rubber Mallet: For seating stubborn Storz couplings that won't seat by hand.

Rope to Move Hose: A loop of utility rope used to drag charged hose that's too heavy and pressurized to move by hand alone.
Why It's Needed
The hydrant operation has to be fast and reliable. You cannot fight fire without water, and water comes from the hydrant. Every item in this bag solves a specific problem that occurs regularly in hydrant operations. Knowing the bag cold — knowing where each item is without looking — is a fundamental skill.
Also Known As
Hydrant bag, hypo bag, supply bag, hydrant kit.
🔰 ROOKIE MISTAKE
Forgetting to take the hydrant bag when walking the hydrant. You'll get to the hydrant and have nothing to open it with. The bag goes with you — every time, no exceptions.

Compartment 2 — Irons, Forcible Entry & Air Bags

Driver's Side · Ground-level access · Forcible entry tools + extrication
Halligan Bar (Haligan)
What It Is
A heavy-duty steel bar approximately 30" long with three working ends: a fork (adz-like flat hook) on one end, a pick at a 90-degree angle to the fork, and a curved claw-fork on the opposite end. Designed by FDNY Chief Hugh Halligan in the 1940s, it is arguably the most important forcible entry tool ever made. It is almost always used as a matched pair with the flat-head axe — together this pair is called "the irons."
How to Use It
  1. Gap the door: Strike the fork end (adz end) into the door frame between the door and the jamb with the flat-head axe.
  2. Set the fork: Rotate the bar so the fork tines straddle the door frame around the locking bolt/latch area.
  3. Force the door: Apply rotational leverage by pushing the bar toward the door — the fork pries the door away from the jamb and defeats the lock.
  4. Pick end: Used to punch through doors, pry padlocks, or gain purchase in small openings.
  5. Claw end: Used for pulling ceilings, prying stubborn materials, and ventilation.
Why It's Needed
Rapid entry to locked or obstructed structures saves lives. Time spent fighting a door is time the fire is growing. The Halligan is designed to defeat outswing and inswing doors with threaded or mortise locks in seconds with the correct technique.
Also Known As
Halligan bar, haligan, hooligan tool, the bar, pro-bar, the irons (when paired with axe), the set.
⚠ CRITICAL SAFETY
Before forcing entry: radio the IC, confirm you have the right address, and do a quick size-up of the door (outswing or inswing?). Forcing entry on the wrong side of a door or into a wind-driven fire scenario can create a blowtorch condition that kills the entry team. Know your door before you force it.
▶ WATCH IT IN ACTION
YouTube: "Halligan irons forcible entry training"
Flat Head Axe
What It Is
A firefighting axe with a flat striking face on one side and a sharpened axe blade on the other. The flat face is used as a striking tool (driving the Halligan). The blade is used for cutting, prying, and creating ventilation openings. This is the companion to the Halligan — "the irons" means this axe + the Halligan bar, working together.
How to Use It
  1. When working irons, the axe-person drives the Halligan fork into the door gap using the flat-head striking face.
  2. Once set, the axe person either assists with prying force or provides lighting and protection for the Halligan operator.
  3. As a cutting tool, use controlled, aimed swings for ventilation holes, wood cutting, or door panel removal.
  4. Always keep the blade covered when not in use — axe bags or sheaths prevent injury during transport.
Why It's Needed
The Halligan cannot drive itself into a door gap. The flat-head axe is the force multiplier that makes the Halligan work. Together, one team of two firefighters with irons can force most residential doors in under 30 seconds.
Also Known As
Flat-head axe, flat head, fire axe, FHA, the axe (when carried with Halligan), part of "the irons."
Maul (Sledgehammer)
What It Is
A large-headed, heavy striking tool — essentially a heavy hammer. In the fire service, the maul is used as an alternative to the flat-head axe for driving the Halligan when extreme force is required, or for breaching walls, breaking through security doors and commercial steel doors, and other high-force impact work.
How to Use It
Hold the handle with two hands, use controlled swings to drive the Halligan or strike materials. Do not swing overhead in low-ceiling spaces — you'll hit the ceiling and lose control of the tool. Stay square to the target for accuracy.
Why It's Needed
Commercial structures, security doors, and padlocked gates require more force than a flat-head axe can deliver. The maul also pairs with the Halligan on heavy-duty forcible entry scenarios where a standard axe strike isn't getting the bar set.
Also Known As
Maul, sledge, sledgehammer, force maul, striking tool.
Closet Pike Pole
What It Is
A short-handled pike pole (typically 4–6 feet) designed to be used in confined spaces like closets, attic access areas, and smaller rooms where a full-length pike pole would be unmanageable. It has the same metal head as a regular pike pole: a sharp point and a sharpened hook.
How to Use It
Use the point to punch into ceilings, walls, and floors to check for hidden fire extension. Use the hook to pull down ceiling materials, lath and plaster, or drywall to expose concealed spaces. In overhaul, systematically open concealed spaces — fire travels in walls, floors, and attic spaces.
Why It's Needed
Fire hides. A fully knocked-down fire that has extended into a wall or ceiling cavity will re-ignite hours later if not fully opened up and extinguished. The closet pike pole is your tool for checking small, confined areas without needing full extension.
Also Known As
Short pike pole, closet hook, drywall hook, overhaul hook, short hook.
Bolt Cutters — Medium & Large ×1 each
What They Are
Heavy-duty cutting tools with compound-action pivot systems and hardened steel cutting jaws. The medium cutter has a hook feature for catching and cutting barbed wire. The large cutter is for heavy-gauge padlocks, chain-link fence, and heavy chains. Stored on the inside of the swinging door of Comp 2.
How to Use Them
  1. Position the jaws around the material to be cut — engage as close to the pivot as possible for maximum leverage (the closer to the pivot, the more force the jaws apply).
  2. Apply firm, steady squeezing pressure — avoid jerking or twisting which can damage the jaws.
  3. For hardened padlock shackles, cut both sides of the shackle rather than the body.
  4. For barbed wire, use the hook on the medium cutter to capture the wire before cutting.
Why They're Needed
Access control. Locked gates, chained fences, padlocked doors, and security cables are common barriers between you and victims. Bolt cutters are the fastest non-powered solution for most lock and chain scenarios.
Also Known As
Bolt cutters, bolt croppers, chain cutters, lock cutters.
⚠ CRITICAL SAFETY
Do not attempt to cut electrical cable, energized wire, or anything you can't positively identify as non-electrical. Always assume any cable near a structure fire or rescue may be energized until confirmed otherwise by the utility or a meter reading.
K-Tool in Leather Case
What It Is
The K-Tool is a specialized lock-pulling device shaped like the letter K. It's inserted over a commercial door lock cylinder, locked in place, and then driven by the Halligan bar to extract (pull) the entire lock cylinder from the door — exposing the locking mechanism and allowing the door to be opened without major damage to the door itself.
How to Use It
  1. Slide the K-Tool over the lock cylinder — it grips the cylinder on two sides.
  2. Hook the Halligan bar's fork end through the K-Tool's drive ring.
  3. Apply outward (pulling) force with the Halligan while a second firefighter strikes the Halligan from behind with the flat-head axe.
  4. The cylinder pulls out, exposing the tumbler mechanism.
  5. Use a screwdriver or the pick of the Halligan to actuate the exposed mechanism and open the lock.
Why It's Needed
Commercial structures with high-security lock cylinders (mortise locks, deadbolts, Medeco-type locks) resist conventional door forcing. The K-Tool defeats these by removing the cylinder rather than forcing the bolt — faster and with less collateral damage on non-fire entries.
Also Known As
K-tool, key tool, cylinder puller, lock puller.

Air Bags — Lifting Bags ×1–2 in Comp 2
What They Are
High-pressure pneumatic lifting bags made of reinforced neoprene or similar material. When inflated, they generate enormous lifting force — some bags can lift 20–75 tons depending on size. They are inflated from SCBA air tanks or a dedicated air supply and controlled by the orange controller box (stored in Comp 3). On E62, both bags may occasionally be in Comp 2 rather than Comp 3. Max inflation pressure: 118 PSI — do not exceed.
How to Use Them
  1. Size up the load and determine bag placement — bags should be inserted into openings at least as wide as the deflated bag thickness.
  2. Insert bags into the void space under the load.
  3. Connect the air supply hose to the bag and to the orange controller.
  4. Inflate slowly in small increments — never inflate beyond 118 PSI.
  5. Crib as you go — place cribbing (wooden blocks, see Comp 3) under the load as it rises.
  6. Never work under a load supported only by air bags — always have cribbing in place.
Why They're Needed
Vehicle extrication, structural collapse rescue, and lifting heavy objects off trapped victims. A car can weigh 3,000–5,000 lbs. A collapsed floor section can pin a victim under hundreds of pounds. Air bags provide controlled, safe, powerful lifting without power equipment.
Also Known As
Air bags, lifting bags, high-pressure bags, pneumatic bags, rescue bags.
⚠ CRITICAL SAFETY — NEVER EXCEED 118 PSI
Overinflating an air bag can cause catastrophic bag failure, ejecting the load and injuring rescuers. The 118 PSI limit is a hard safety ceiling — both E61 and E62 use this same limit. Also: never stack more than two bags directly on top of each other — unstacked bags are unstable and can eject sideways under load.

Air Tanks ×3 — Outside of Comp 2
What They Are

Cellar Nozzle on Cellar Pipe
What It Is
A specialized nozzle assembly designed for fighting basement and cellar fires from the floor above. The cellar pipe is a curved metal tube that gets inserted through a small hole cut in the floor. The cellar nozzle at its end spins (driven by water pressure) and sprays water in a 360-degree pattern, flooding the space below without requiring anyone to descend into the fire area.
How to Use It
  1. Cut or locate a small access hole in the floor above the basement fire.
  2. Lower the cellar pipe and nozzle through the hole.
  3. Connect your attack line to the inlet of the cellar pipe.
  4. Flow water — the spinning nozzle disperses water throughout the cellar space.
  5. Do not advance into the cellar until the fire is knocked down and conditions are evaluated.
Why It's Needed
Basement fires are among the most dangerous scenarios in residential firefighting. Fire burning below the floor you're standing on is burning through your means of support. A cellar nozzle lets you apply water to a below-grade fire without sending firefighters into a potentially floor-collapse scenario.
Also Known As
Cellar nozzle, cellar pipe, rotary nozzle, whirling nozzle, basement nozzle.
⚠ CRITICAL SAFETY
Basement fires demand extreme caution about floor integrity. If the fire has been burning long enough to char the joists supporting your floor, that floor can fail suddenly. Sound the floor before advancing — use your tool, your foot, and your eyes. The cellar nozzle exists precisely to let you fight this fire without betting your life on a compromised floor.

BOTTOM SHELF
Ventilation Fan (Positive Pressure Ventilation)
What It Is
A large, high-powered electric or gas-powered blower fan used to force fresh air into a structure and push smoke, heat, and toxic gases out. This is called Positive Pressure Ventilation (PPV) or Positive Pressure Attack (PPA). The fan is placed outside a doorway and angled to cover the entire door opening, creating positive air pressure inside the structure that pushes contaminated atmosphere out through an exhaust opening on the opposite side.
How to Use It
  1. Identify your inlet (entry door) and exhaust opening (window, vent, opening on opposite side).
  2. Place the fan approximately 8–10 feet from the door opening, angled to cover the full doorframe — the air cone should slightly exceed the door perimeter.
  3. Open the exhaust opening on the far side of the structure before starting the fan.
  4. Start the fan and confirm positive pressure — smoke should move toward the exhaust.
  5. Coordinate fan operation with your IC and attack team.
Why It's Needed
Smoke is toxic, hot, and obscures visibility. PPV removes it faster and more completely than traditional hydraulic or natural ventilation. It improves conditions for crews, improves victim survivability, and dramatically reduces property damage by clearing smoke from uninvolved areas.
Also Known As
PPV fan, positive pressure fan, smoke fan, electric fan, ventilation fan, blower, the fan.
⚠ CRITICAL SAFETY
Never use PPV on an uncontrolled fire without IC approval. Adding fresh oxygen to a compartment fire can intensify the fire rapidly. PPV is a coordinated tactic — used improperly it can push fire toward victims or crews. Also: confirm the fan is plugged into the apparatus charging circuit during non-use so it starts when you need it.
Orange Box — Air Bag Controller
What It Is
The control manifold for the pneumatic air bags. It's a regulator-and-valve assembly (typically orange-colored for visibility) that connects your air supply (SCBA tank) to one or two air bags, allowing controlled, regulated inflation. It includes pressure gauges, individual bag valves, and a safety relief valve.
How to Use It
  1. Connect the SCBA cylinder to the controller's inlet fitting.
  2. Connect the air bags to the outlet ports — each bag has its own hose and port.
  3. Open the SCBA cylinder valve — watch the supply gauge rise.
  4. Open bag valves slowly and in small increments — inflate bags a few inches at a time while cribbing underneath.
  5. Monitor bag pressure gauges — never exceed 118 PSI.
  6. To deflate: close the bag valve and open the relief/exhaust.
Why It's Needed
You cannot safely inflate air bags without a regulator. Direct SCBA tank pressure (4,500 PSI) connected directly to an air bag would rupture it instantly. The controller steps pressure down and gives you precise control over inflation rate and pressure.
Also Known As
Air bag controller, control box, manifold, regulator, orange box.
Cribbing
What It Is
Short lengths of hardwood timber (typically 4×4 or 2×4, cut to specific lengths) used to build stable, stacking supports under lifted or displaced loads during rescue operations. Cribbing goes hand-in-hand with air bags and hydraulic tools — as you lift, you crib. The load is always supported by cribbing, never by equipment alone.
How to Use It
Build a "box crib" by alternating layers of wood at 90-degree angles, like a log cabin — this creates maximum stability and bearing surface. As a load is lifted an inch, slide cribbing underneath. Never allow the load to be fully supported only by air bags or jacks — always have cribbing at every lift increment.
Why It's Needed
Air bags can fail. Hydraulic jacks can leak down. A vehicle can shift. If any of these happen with a victim under a load and no cribbing in place, the result is fatal. Cribbing is the passive, fail-safe support that protects the victim and the rescuers regardless of equipment failure.
Also Known As
Cribbing, crib blocks, shoring blocks, timber crib, step chocks (step-shaped pre-cut wedge version).
⚠ CRITICAL SAFETY — "CRIB AS YOU GO"
This phrase should be burned into your brain. Every half-inch of lift gets a half-inch of crib placed under the load before the next lift. There is no acceptable exception to this. A rescuer who skips cribbing to "get it done faster" is gambling with a victim's life.
Transformer Nozzles
What They Are
Specialized nozzles designed to spray water into small openings or enclosed spaces — think: injection firefighting. They typically attach to a standard hose coupling and allow water to be directed into a wall cavity, ceiling void, locked room, or any space too small or too dangerous to enter. Also called "penetrating nozzles" or "piercing nozzles" in some configurations.
How to Use Them
Insert the nozzle tip into the target opening (or drill/punch a hole first). Connect the attack line and flow water into the concealed space. The nozzle distributes water throughout the void to knock down hidden fire. Particularly effective in wall fires, concealed ceiling fires, and attic fires where structural penetration is not needed.
Why They're Needed
Opening walls with tools is time-consuming and creates structural damage. Where possible, injecting water through a small hole is faster and less destructive while still knocking down hidden fire extension.
Also Known As
Transformer nozzle, piercing nozzle, penetrating nozzle, injection nozzle, Elkhart nozzle, Hi-Combat nozzle.

Hurst Rescue Cutter & Hurst Rescue Spreader
What They Are
Hurst Spreader ("The Jaws"): Hydraulically-powered tool with two blades that spread apart or squeeze together with tremendous force. Used to pry open vehicle doors, displace steering wheels, and create access to trapped vehicle occupants.

Hurst Cutter ("The Cutters"): Hydraulically-powered cutting tool — essentially industrial scissors — used to cut vehicle A, B, and C pillars, door hinges, seat belts, and structural elements of vehicle bodies. Together, the spreader creates space and the cutter removes obstructions.
How to Use Them

Why They're Needed
Modern vehicle construction — high-strength steel, airbags, complex geometry — makes manual extrication virtually impossible in serious crashes. Hurst tools are purpose-designed to cut and spread vehicle structures that no manual tool can defeat.
Also Known As

Hydraulic Ram
What It Is
A hydraulic cylinder that extends under power to push or spread materials apart. Where the spreader pries in a V-shape, the ram pushes in a linear direction — it can displace a steering column, push a firewall back, or force structural elements apart that are too restricted for the spreader tips to fit into.
How to Use It
Connect to the hydraulic power unit. Position the ram base against a solid structural anchor and the extension tip against the target. Extend slowly with controlled force. Use the Quick Kick ram support (silver bracket with teeth) to hold the ram stable when positioning in a vehicle or structure without a natural anchor point.
Why It's Needed
Not every rescue can be solved with spreading or cutting. Sometimes you need directed linear force — pushing a dashboard off a trapped leg, pushing a door frame away from a pinned door, or extending space along a single axis. The ram fills this gap.
Also Known As
Ram, hydraulic ram, push ram, power ram, porta-power (informal for general hydraulic tools).
Reciprocating Saw
What It Is
A battery or electric-powered saw with a blade that moves in a reciprocating (back-and-forth) motion. Unlike a circular saw (round blade spinning) or chainsaw, the reciprocating saw is highly maneuverable, operates in confined spaces, and can cut wood, metal, plastic, and composite materials depending on blade selection.
How to Use It
  1. Select correct blade for the material — wood blades, metal blades, and combination blades are different.
  2. Insert the blade fully and confirm it's locked before operation.
  3. Start the saw before contacting the material — contact starting can bind or kick back.
  4. Use steady forward pressure — let the blade do the work, don't force it.
  5. Keep your free hand clear of the blade path at all times.
Why It's Needed
Rescue situations involving seatbelts, aircraft skin, vehicle floors, structural lumber, and security bars all require rapid cutting. The reciprocating saw handles most of these. It's also lighter and more maneuverable than a chainsaw in confined rescue environments.
Also Known As
Reciprocating saw, Sawzall (Milwaukee trade name — extremely common in fire service), recip saw, demo saw, sabre saw.
Window Punch
What It Is
A small spring-loaded punch device — the size of a large marker — with a hardened steel tip. When pressed against a vehicle's tempered glass window (side or rear windows, not laminated windshield) and the mechanism is activated, it releases a concentrated impact that causes the glass to shatter into thousands of small, relatively harmless pieces.
How to Use It
  1. Position the tip firmly in one of the lower corners of the tempered glass window.
  2. Push firmly to activate the spring mechanism — the tip strikes the glass with concentrated force.
  3. The glass will "dice" into small cubes — use a gloved hand or tool to clear the remaining glass.
  4. Clear the frame of glass shards before reaching inside near the victim.
Why It's Needed
Speed. Manual window breaking with a tool risks injuring the victim inside with flying glass or tool entry. A window punch breaks tempered glass cleanly and quickly in any confined space. Essential for vehicle rescue and locked vehicle/child entrapment calls.
Also Known As
Window punch, life hammer, glass punch, spring punch, center punch, ResQMe (popular trade name).
🔰 ROOKIE MISTAKE
Trying to use the window punch on a laminated windshield — it won't work. Windshields are laminated safety glass, which holds together when broken. Window punches only work on tempered glass (side and rear windows). Use the Halligan, axe, or saw on windshields.

RASP — 200 ft Search Rope
What It Is
RASP (Rapid Anchor Search Package) or search rope — a 200-foot guideline rope for interior search operations in zero-visibility environments. The rope has beads at regular intervals and a butterfly knot at 100 feet, allowing firefighters to maintain orientation, track their distance of travel, and communicate their position to crew members outside.
How to Use It
  1. Anchor the bag end (the starting end) at a fixed point at the entry — a doorknob, stair railing, or structural anchor.
  2. The search team pays out rope as they advance into the structure.
  3. The bead markers at intervals tell you how far you've traveled from the anchor.
  4. The butterfly knot at 100 ft is a tactile signal in the dark — "you're halfway."
  5. To return, follow the rope back to the anchor — never let go of the rope in zero visibility.
Why It's Needed
Search operations in zero-visibility smoke conditions are disorienting even for experienced firefighters. The search rope gives you a physical lifeline to follow back out. Firefighters have died of disorientation in smoke-filled buildings — the search rope is a critical life-safety tool.

⚠ CRITICAL SAFETY
The rope is only useful if it's anchored before you advance. A common error is walking the rope in without securing the anchor end — now you have a coil of rope in the building and no way out. Anchor first, advance second. Every time.

BOTTOM SHELF
Red Bag — 4 PFDs + 1 Throw Preserver
What They Are
PFD (Personal Flotation Device): Life vests/jackets that keep a person afloat in water. Four are stored for crew use in water rescue scenarios.

Throw Preserver / Ring Buoy: A ring-shaped flotation device attached to a throw line. Thrown to a distressed swimmer or victim, they grab it and you pull them to safety — without entering the water yourself.
How to Use Them
PFD: Put it on before entering or near the water — not after you've fallen in. Fasten all buckles and adjust for snug fit.

Throw Preserver: Hold one end of the line, throw the ring past the victim (not at them), and pull the line to bring them in. Aim to land the ring beyond the victim so the rope crosses their body.
Why They're Needed
Water rescue is a significant risk in any district near bodies of water, flooding, or swimming pools. Swift water, cold water, and current all make drowning a real risk to rescuers as well as victims. The PFD doesn't make you a rescue swimmer — it keeps you alive if you fall in.
Also Known As
PFD, life jacket, life vest, floatie (informal). Ring buoy: life ring, throw ring, throw preserver, toss rope.
⚠ CRITICAL SAFETY — REACH, THROW, ROW, GO
Water rescue follows a priority order: (1) Reach from shore, (2) Throw a line or ring, (3) Row or use a boat, (4) Go in as a last resort with a PFD. Do not enter swift or cold water without your department's water rescue training and proper equipment. More rescuers have drowned attempting water rescue than the original victims.
W-Tool (Hydraulic Spreading Tool)
What It Is
The W-Tool (also called a "battering ram" in battery-powered versions) is a hydraulic tool designed to spread door jambs — forcing space in the gap between a door and its frame. It can also be used as a battering ram to force doors. One end drives into the door gap; the W-shape applies spreading force to the jamb.
How to Use It

Mustang Suit
What It Is
A marine-grade immersion suit (survival suit) that provides both thermal protection and buoyancy to a person in cold water. "Mustang" is a major manufacturer of marine immersion suits — the name has become genericized in many fire departments. These bright orange or red suits provide hypothermia protection for extended periods in cold water and keep the wearer afloat without effort.
How to Use It
  1. Sit down if possible while donning — these suits are bulky.
  2. Step both feet into the legs and pull the suit up over your turnout gear or uniform.
  3. Slide both arms in and zip the front closure fully.
  4. Secure the hood, wrist closures, and any remaining fasteners.
  5. The built-in buoyancy will keep you face-up in water even if unconscious.
Why It's Needed
Cold water kills through hypothermia and incapacitation within minutes. A firefighter who enters or falls into cold water without thermal protection loses fine motor control within 3–5 minutes and can lose consciousness within 30 minutes. The Mustang suit extends survival time dramatically for rescuers operating in or near cold water.
Also Known As
Mustang suit, immersion suit, survival suit, dry suit (though a true dry suit is different), floatation suit.
⚠ CRITICAL SAFETY
The Mustang suit provides buoyancy but is not a substitute for training. If you are not trained in water rescue, do not enter the water — even suited up. Know the "Reach, Throw, Row, Go" priority order. Your suit keeps you alive if you go in accidentally; it doesn't make you a trained rescue swimmer.
Rope Throw Bag
What It Is
A bag pre-packed with a floating throw rope (typically 50–75 feet of ¼" polypropylene rope that floats). You throw the bag — not just the rope — to a victim in the water. The bag acts as a projectile and the rope pays out freely from the bag as it flies through the air.
How to Use It
  1. Open the bag top and confirm the rope is loaded and the free end is in your hand.
  2. Stand upstream of the victim in moving water.
  3. Swing the bag underhand and release — aim to land the bag just past the victim so the rope crosses them.
  4. Shout "ROPE!" as you throw — victim should grab the rope, not the bag.
  5. Brace yourself and let the victim pendulum to shore on the rope.
  6. To repack: stuff the rope back in the bag loosely — do not coil it, or it will tangle on the next throw.
Why It's Needed
Fastest water rescue tool available for a shore-based rescuer. A throw bag can reach a victim 50–70 feet away in 2 seconds. Compare that to any other option and the value is obvious.
Also Known As
Throw bag, rescue throw bag, rope bag, toss bag, Z-Rig bag (when configured for mechanical advantage).
Water Helmet
What It Is
A water rescue helmet — designed with ventilation, a low profile, and secure fastening for water entry and current navigation. Unlike a structural firefighting helmet, a water rescue helmet won't trap water and drag you under, and is designed to stay on in current.
Why It's Needed
Head protection during water rescue operations. Swift water currents drive rescuers into rocks, debris, bridge supports, and other structures. A water rescue helmet is the appropriate PPE for this environment — your structural helmet is not.
Also Known As
Water rescue helmet, swift water helmet, rescue helmet.

Compartment 7 — Fire Extinguishers & Spill Control

Passenger's Side · All four extinguisher types + EV fire blanket
💡 THE FOUR CLASSES OF FIRE — REVIEW
Class A = Ordinary combustibles (wood, paper, fabric). Class B = Flammable liquids/gases. Class C = Electrical equipment (energized). Class D = Combustible metals. Class K = Cooking oils/fats. Know which extinguisher matches which class.
Silver Bullet — Pressurized Water Extinguisher
What It Is
A pressurized stainless steel cylinder (the "silver bullet" — named for its appearance) containing plain water under air pressure. Effective only on Class A fires (ordinary combustibles — wood, paper, fabric). No surfactant, no foam — just water.
How to Use It
Pull the pin, aim the nozzle at the base of the fire, squeeze the handle, and sweep side-to-side. Capacity is approximately 2.5 gallons — enough for a small Class A fire. Not a substitute for a hose line.
Why It's Needed
Small smoldering fires (trash can, weed fire at the base of a building) can be hit with the silver bullet without deploying a full hose line. Fast, clean, no secondary contamination, no residue.
Also Known As
Silver bullet, water can, pressurized water, 2½-gallon water can.
⚠ CRITICAL SAFETY
Never use a water extinguisher on electrical fires (Class C) or flammable liquid fires (Class B). Water conducts electricity and will spread burning liquid fires. Know your agent and know your fire class.
ABC Dry Chemical Extinguisher
What It Is
A pressurized cylinder containing monoammonium phosphate (dry chemical powder). The "ABC" rating means it's effective on Class A (combustibles), Class B (flammable liquids), and Class C (energized electrical) fires. It suppresses fire by interrupting the chemical chain reaction.
How to Use It
PASS: Pull the pin, Aim at the base of the fire, Squeeze the handle, Sweep from side to side. Use in short bursts, starting from the near edge of the fire and sweeping through. Stay low, stay upwind.
Why It's Needed
Versatility. One extinguisher handles three classes. Ideal for vehicle fires, fuel-related fires, and any environment with multiple potential fire types. The most common extinguisher type you'll encounter.
Also Known As
ABC extinguisher, dry chem, dry chemical, monoammonium phosphate, the purple K (different dry chem specifically for Class B).
🔰 ROOKIE MISTAKE
Emptying the whole extinguisher in one shot and running out before the fire's knocked down. Use short controlled bursts. Dry chemical depletes fast and you can't reload on scene.
CO₂ Extinguisher
What It Is
A high-pressure cylinder of liquid carbon dioxide that converts to gas when discharged, smothering the fire by displacing oxygen and cooling the fuel. Effective on Class B (flammable liquids) and Class C (electrical) fires. Leaves no residue — critical for protecting sensitive electrical equipment and electronics.
How to Use It
PASS: Pull, Aim (hold the horn/nozzle — it will get extremely cold), Squeeze, Sweep. Keep distance — CO₂ discharges at -109°F and the horn can cause frostbite. Do not use in small enclosed spaces without ventilation — CO₂ depletes oxygen and can incapacitate rescuers.
Why It's Needed
Electrical panel fires, server rooms, electrical equipment fires. CO₂ leaves no residue and won't damage equipment the way dry chemical will. For any fire in or near sensitive electronics, CO₂ is the preferred agent.
Also Known As
CO₂ extinguisher, carbon dioxide extinguisher, the CO₂.
⚠ CRITICAL SAFETY — FROSTBITE RISK
The discharge horn on a CO₂ extinguisher reaches -109°F. Never grab the horn bare-handed. Wear gloves. The cold gas discharge also looks alarming — fire is still present even as you're applying CO₂. Don't stop before the fire is confirmed out.
Class K Extinguisher
What It Is
A wet chemical extinguisher specifically designed for cooking oil and fat fires (Class K). Contains potassium acetate, potassium citrate, or potassium carbonate solutions that react with the cooking oil to form a soapy foam (saponification), effectively smothering the fire and preventing re-ignition.
How to Use It
Apply gently and from a distance — do not spray directly onto burning oil at close range, which can cause splashing and spread the fire. Move the nozzle side-to-side in a sweeping motion across the oil surface. Allow the foam blanket to form and smother.
Why It's Needed
Commercial kitchen fires are common and extremely dangerous. A deep fryer or commercial range fire at full temperature (400°F+) will re-ignite if you don't permanently lower the temperature of the oil below its auto-ignition point. Class K agents are specifically formulated to achieve this where ABC dry chemical or CO₂ fail to prevent re-ignition.
Also Known As
Class K, wet chemical, kitchen extinguisher, Ansul R-102 (common commercial suppression system brand).
Kitty Litter
What It Is
Plain clay or absorbent granular material — yes, literally commercial kitty litter or an equivalent absorbent granule. Used as a spill absorbent for small petroleum product spills (fuel, oil, hydraulic fluid) on roadways or incident scenes.
How to Use It
Spread the granules over the spill, allow it to absorb the liquid (a few minutes), then sweep or shovel the saturated material into a bag or container for disposal. Temporary traction for slick roadway patches can also be created with dry application.
Why It's Needed
Vehicle accident scenes routinely produce fuel and fluid spills that create both fire risk and traffic hazard. Kitty litter is the field expedient solution for small spills without waiting for a hazmat team.
Also Known As
Kitty litter, absorbent granules, floor dry, Oil-Dri (brand name), absorbent, Speedy Dry.
Fire Blanket — For Electric Vehicles
What It Is
A large, fire-resistant or fire-suppressing blanket specifically designed to be draped over a burning electric vehicle (EV). EV lithium-ion battery fires undergo "thermal runaway" — a self-sustaining chemical reaction that generates its own oxygen and cannot be extinguished with conventional water or dry chemical suppression. The blanket works by containing the heat and limiting oxygen to slow/halt the reaction, and by containing the fire to prevent spread to surrounding vehicles or structures.
How to Use It
  1. Ensure no occupants remain in the vehicle (or perform extraction before blanketing).
  2. Unfold the blanket and approach from upwind.
  3. Drape the blanket completely over the burning EV, covering as much surface as possible.
  4. Continue to monitor — EV thermal runaway can reignite or break through containment.
  5. Large volumes of cooling water into the battery compartment remain the primary suppression method; the blanket is a containment/spread-control tool.
Why It's Needed
EV fires are an emerging and growing challenge for the fire service. Lithium-ion battery thermal runaway can require 3,000+ gallons of water to suppress, can take hours to extinguish, and can reignite hours or days later. The blanket is a practical, field-deployable tool to contain the hazard and prevent fire spread while managing the incident.
Also Known As
EV fire blanket, electric vehicle blanket, battery fire blanket, thermal containment blanket.
⚠ CRITICAL SAFETY — EV FIRES
Do not assume an EV fire is out just because flames are no longer visible. Thermal runaway can continue internally and reignite violently hours later. EV incidents require extended monitoring and documentation. Your turnout gear does NOT protect against the toxic gases (hydrogen fluoride, etc.) released in lithium-ion battery fires — stay upwind and in SCBA whenever working near burning EVs.

4-Gas CO Meter (O₂, CO, H₂S, Combustible Gas)
What It Is
A four-sensor electronic atmospheric monitor that simultaneously detects and measures: Oxygen (O₂) level, Carbon Monoxide (CO), Hydrogen Sulfide (H₂S), and combustible/flammable gas (typically measured as LEL — Lower Explosive Limit percentage). This is your primary atmospheric testing tool before and during entry into potentially hazardous atmospheres.
How to Use It
  1. Turn on and allow to calibrate in fresh air — do not start in a contaminated atmosphere.
  2. Test the atmosphere BEFORE entry — poke the sampling probe through a keyhole, gap, or small opening first.
  3. Normal readings: O₂ 20.9%, CO 0 ppm, H₂S 0 ppm, LEL 0%.
  4. Alarm thresholds: CO >35 ppm (TWA), O₂ <19.5% or >23.5%, H₂S >10 ppm, LEL >10%.
  5. Never bypass or ignore alarms — the meter detects what your senses cannot.
Why It's Needed
CO is odorless and colorless. H₂S smells like rotten eggs at low levels but paralyzes your sense of smell at higher concentrations. Oxygen deficiency causes rapid incapacitation without warning. Combustible gas at 10% LEL is 10% of the way to an explosion. The meter detects all of these — your nose and eyes do not.
Also Known As
4-gas meter, multi-gas meter, CGI (combustible gas indicator), MSA (brand name), RAE meter, atmospheric tester, gas monitor.
⚠ CRITICAL SAFETY
Always bump-test your meter before every shift. A bump test (brief exposure to a known test gas concentration) confirms all four sensors are working. A meter that hasn't been bump-tested may show clean readings in a contaminated atmosphere and kill you. This is not optional.
Gas Tracker / Methane Meter (with HCN Sensor)
What It Is
A dedicated combustible gas detector optimized for natural gas (methane/CH₄) detection, with an additional Hydrogen Cyanide (HCN) sensor in the bottom right corner of the device. HCN is produced in almost every structural fire from burning synthetics, foam, and plastics. It is one of the primary killers in smoke inhalation — more dangerous than CO in many fire scenarios.
How to Use It
For gas investigations: sweep the meter through the space and watch the combustible gas percentage. Check low (gas is heavier than air for many fuels) and high (methane is lighter — it accumulates near the ceiling). For HCN monitoring during post-fire operations: the HCN reading alerts you to dangerous cyanide concentrations in smoke/overhaul environments.
Why It's Needed
HCN is a fire service silent killer. Modern building contents — foam furniture, synthetic carpets, plastics, composite materials — all off-gas hydrogen cyanide when they burn. Without HCN monitoring, firefighters and patients are exposed to concentrations that are immediately dangerous without realizing it. The 4-gas meter may not have a dedicated HCN sensor — this device fills that gap.
Also Known As
Gas tracker, methane meter, combustible gas indicator, HCN meter, cyanide meter.
⚠ CRITICAL SAFETY — HCN AWARENESS
HCN has an IDLH (Immediately Dangerous to Life and Health) of just 50 ppm. Smoke from a typical house fire can contain 50–300 ppm of HCN. This is why you never, ever take your mask off inside a fire building or during active overhaul — even when flames are out, HCN and other toxins remain.
Hot Stick
What It Is
A non-contact voltage detector on a long insulated pole — essentially a voltage sensor mounted on a fiberglass extension stick. When held near energized conductors or electrical components, it buzzes or lights up to indicate voltage presence without requiring physical contact.
How to Use It
Extend the hot stick toward the suspected conductor or electrical component from a safe distance. If the meter activates (light, buzz, alarm), the object is energized — do not touch, do not get closer, notify your officer, and request utility company response.
Why It's Needed
Downed power lines, vehicle accidents involving utility poles, and fires near electrical infrastructure are extremely hazardous. The hot stick lets you test electrical status from a safe distance before committing personnel to the immediate hazard area.
Also Known As
Hot stick, voltage detector, tic tracer, non-contact voltage tester, sonar stick, electrical tester.

AED — Automated External Defibrillator
What It Is
A computerized device that analyzes a cardiac patient's heart rhythm, determines if a shockable rhythm (ventricular fibrillation or ventricular tachycardia) is present, and delivers a controlled electrical shock to attempt to restore a normal rhythm. The AED talks you through the process — it literally tells you what to do.
How to Use It
  1. Power on the AED — it will begin verbal instructions immediately.
  2. Apply pads as shown in the diagram: one below the right collarbone, one on the left lateral chest wall below the armpit.
  3. Ensure all personnel are clear of the patient — shout "CLEAR!"
  4. Follow the AED's instructions for shock delivery or continued CPR.
  5. Resume high-quality CPR immediately after the shock — do not wait to check for a pulse.
Why It's Needed
Early defibrillation is one of the highest-impact interventions in cardiac arrest. For every minute of VF without defibrillation, survival decreases 7–10%. As the first arriving unit, your AED may arrive before ALS — it saves lives when used immediately.
Also Known As
AED, defibrillator, defib, cardiac monitor (when a full 12-lead monitor is carried), the AED.
⚠ CRITICAL SAFETY
Do not touch the patient during AED rhythm analysis or shock delivery. Ensure all personnel are visually confirmed clear before the shock. Post-shock, immediately resume CPR — do not interrupt compressions to check for a pulse until the AED or your officer instructs otherwise.

What It Is

Why It's Needed

  • Dawn Soap and Brush in Bucket: For cleaning hose, equipment, and the apparatus itself. Also used as a foam additive for certain wetting applications on Class A materials (though Class A foam concentrate is preferred). The bucket and brush are the end-of-shift cleanup kit.
⚠ DECK GUN / MASTER STREAM SAFETY
When the deck gun is flowing, no one enters the building. Master streams are a defensive operation. The pressure and flow a master stream delivers will injure firefighters inside, cause structural compromise, and reduce steam-burn survivability. When the deck gun goes to work, interior crews come out. No exceptions.

06.05 Jumper Cables aka: Booster Cables, Jump Cables
Color Code
Red = Positive (+)  |  Black = Negative (−)
For the dead car: red cable goes on the ground (negative terminal) as the final connection to avoid sparks near the battery.
Proper Connection Sequence
  1. Red to dead battery (+)
  2. Red to good battery (+)
  3. Black to good battery (−)
  4. Black to ground on dead car (not the dead battery) — unpainted metal, away from battery
  5. Start the good car, then the dead car
  6. Remove in reverse order
Why Ground, Not Battery?

Batteries off-gas hydrogen — a flammable, explosive gas. Connecting the final clamp directly to the dead battery can spark and ignite that gas. Connecting to an engine ground point (unpainted metal) away from the battery makes that same spark happen safely away from the gas source. This is the step most civilians get wrong. Don't be them.

Acronyms — Know These
AMBU = Artificial Manual Breathing Unit
Used to manually ventilate a patient — the mask you hold to the face to "bag" someone.

BVM = Bag, Valve, Mask
The complete device: squeeze bag → forces air through one-way valve → into mask → into lungs. Used for rescue breathing and assisted ventilation.

OPA = Oropharyngeal Airway (oral)
NPA = Nasopharyngeal Airway (nasal)

Red bag = Trauma (Bleeding, Burns, Broken, Birthing)
Green bag = Airway/Breathing

07.03 Red Bag w/ Black Zipper — Water Rescue aka: Throwbag Bag, Water Rescue Bag
Contents
  • One throw rope (throwbag — rope stuffed in a bag for fast deployment)
  • One life preserver vest (PFD — Personal Flotation Device)
How Throwback Works
  1. Grab the bag, hold the rope end yourself
  2. Throw the bag to the victim — the rope pays out as it travels
  3. Victim grabs the bag/rope — you pull them in
  4. Brace yourself and never let go of your end
"Throwback" = after a rescue, repack the bag by stuffing rope back in (not coiling) for fast re-deployment.
Don't Become the Second Victim

Never enter the water without a PFD and a tethered line. Reach, throw, row, go — in that order. Jumping into water to save someone without equipment has killed more rescuers than victims. Throw the rope first.

07.04 Black Plastic Case — MSA Airpack aka: SCBA, Self-Contained Breathing Apparatus, Air Pack, Scott Pack
Contents
  • One Airpack (SCBA unit)
  • Air bottle (stored in the black case)
  • Medium-sized face piece
What It Is
A self-contained breathing apparatus — a harness-mounted compressed air cylinder with regulator and face piece that provides breathable air in smoke, IDLH (Immediately Dangerous to Life or Health) atmospheres, or oxygen-deficient environments.
Why It's Needed
This is a backup or auxiliary SCBA. An on-scene firefighter who runs low on air, loses their primary SCBA, or needs to stay in the hot zone while their pack is serviced can use this unit. It's also available for situations requiring air in environments not covered by apparatus-mounted SCBAs.
Check the Air Pressure — Every Shift

A low-pressure air bottle in the MSA case is a useless air bottle in an emergency. Verify the cylinder pressure on every shift check. A full bottle reads 4,500 PSI on most modern SCBAs. Below 90% full, flag it for refill.

07.05 Blue Bag w/ Black Straps — Cervical Collar Bag aka: C-Collar Bag, Spinal Immobilization Bag, Stab Kit
Contents
  • Stabilization hardware and two styles of neck braces (cervical collars)
  • Foam head-locks
  • Red/Orange with white stripe bag = Mass Casualties / Triage Tag Bag
  • Orange bag with black zipper = Traction materials (straps, tubes for splints)
Cervical Collar Sizing — Two Methods
Method 1 (Measurement):
Measure with fingers along the blue section of the collar — from bottom of the shoulder blade to the top of the jaw. The number of finger-widths = collar size.

Method 2 (Pin-to-Chin):
Move the black peg into the round hole to form the jaw rest. This is the "pin to chin" collar — the peg gives chin support for proper immobilization.
Foam Head-Locks
Unroll foam blocks on the backboard, position between the board and the patient's head on both sides. Tape across the forehead and across the collar to the board — this completes full spinal immobilization. Without the head blocks, the collar alone doesn't prevent lateral movement.
Triage Tag System — Mass Casualty
The Red/Orange bag with white stripe is your mass casualty triage bag. Uses a 4-color system to classify patient priority:

■ RED — Immediate: life-threatening, can survive with immediate treatment
■ YELLOW — Delayed: serious but stable, can wait
■ GREEN — Minor: "walking wounded," lowest priority
■ BLACK — Expectant: deceased or unsurvivable injuries
C-Collar — Sizing Matters

A cervical collar that's too large won't immobilize. One that's too small can hyperextend the neck and worsen a cervical injury. Take 10 seconds to measure. It changes outcomes. Practice the finger-measurement method on your crew members during downtime so it's automatic on scene.

08.00 ERG — Emergency Response Guidebook aka: ERG, The Orange Book, The Hazmat Bible
What ERG Stands For
Emergency Response Guidebook — published by the U.S. DOT. Your first-responder reference for hazardous materials incidents. It tells you what the hazard is, what to do, how far to stay back, and who to call.
Yellow Pages — By Number
Lists materials in numerical order by UN/NA identification number (4-digit code). Find the number → get the guide number → go to Orange Pages.

Green highlighting in Yellow Pages = Toxic Inhalation Hazard or Dangerous Water Reactive Material. These have evacuation distances in the Green Section.
Blue Pages — By Name
Lists materials alphabetically by name. Same outcome — leads you to the guide number and the Orange Pages.

Green highlighting in Blue Pages = material has evacuation distances in the Green Section.
Orange Pages — Response Guides
The action section. For each guide number:
  • Potential health hazards
  • Fire/explosion risk
  • Emergency response procedures
  • Required PPE
  • Evacuation procedures
  • First aid guidance
  • Spill and fire control measures
Green Pages — Evacuation Distances
For Toxic-by-Inhalation materials. Distances vary by:
  • Small spill vs. Large spill (over 50 gallons = large)
  • Day vs. Night (atmospheric conditions differ)
If you're setting up a perimeter at a hazmat scene, start here for distance guidance.
UN vs. NA Numbers
UN Numbers: International, start with "UN" — used worldwide. Example: UN1203 = Gasoline.

NA Numbers: North America only (USA, Canada, Mexico), start with "NA." Used for materials regulated in North America that may not have an international UN designation. Example: NA1993 = Combustible liquids.
ERG Flowchart — How to Use It
  1. Do you know the UN/NA ID number? → Search Yellow Pages
  2. Do you know the material name? → Search Blue Pages
  3. Do you see a placard or label? → Refer to Pages 8-9
  4. Do you see a rail car or road trailer? → Refer to Pages 10-15
  5. None of the above? → Use Guide 111 (default general hazmat guide)
  6. All roads lead to → Determine the Guide Number → Orange Pages
Back Pages — Hazmat Phone Numbers

The back pages contain critical phone numbers including CHEMTREC (1-800-424-9300) — the 24-hour hazmat emergency hotline. If your ERG doesn't give you what you need, CHEMTREC connects you with chemical manufacturers and hazmat experts in real time. Know this number exists before you need it.

Hot Stick
Voltage Detector / Electrical Safety Rod
▶
An insulated fiberglass pole (4–6 feet) used to safely probe areas near potential electrical hazards without direct contact. Some versions include an audible/visual voltage detection indicator. Allows firefighters to test whether vegetation, fencing, or debris near a power line is energized before touching it.
  1. Extend the hot stick to its full working length.
  2. Approach the suspected hazard from the side — keep your body as far from the hazard as the stick allows.
  3. If voltage-detecting model: hold near (not touching) the suspected conductor and watch/listen for the indicator.
  4. If probing only: use the tip to touch debris near lines — never grab or pull potentially energized material.

Assume every downed power line is live. Current can energize the ground around a downed line — a phenomenon called "step potential." Stay a minimum of one full span (pole-to-pole distance) away. The hot stick reduces risk; it does not eliminate it.
Brush fires regularly down power lines and energize surrounding areas — fences, trees, soil, and standing water can all carry current. You cannot tell if something is energized by looking at it. The hot stick creates a safe buffer between you and potential electrocution.

🔥 Final Notes from Your Mentor

You made it through the whole truck. That's no small thing.

The truck is a system. Water moves from tank → pump → hose → nozzle. Supply moves from hydrant → adapters → pump. Overhaul moves from tools → fire edge → mop-up. When you understand the system, you can improvise when something doesn't go according to plan — and something never goes according to plan.

Know the "why" better than the "what." Anyone can memorize a list of tools. The firefighter who knows why the suction strainer goes 2 feet under, why you never run the pump dry, and why the wheel chock matters on a slope — that firefighter makes better decisions under pressure.

Do your rig check every single shift. Not because someone is watching, but because the moment you skip it is often the moment something's missing. The compartment check is your pre-flight. Pilots don't skip the pre-flight because the last flight went fine.

Ask questions at the tailboard. Every senior firefighter on your crew has a story about each piece of equipment on this truck — a time they needed it, a time it failed, a time they wished they had it. Those stories are worth more than any study guide. Ask for them.
